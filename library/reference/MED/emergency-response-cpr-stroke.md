# Medical & First Aid — Emergency Response, Recognition & Basic CPR

>

> This is emergency reference to act and get help until trained responders arrive. **Take a

---

## 1. The core idea: act, and get help fast

- **Cardiovascular diseases are the leading cause of death globally** — an estimated
  **19.8 million deaths in 2022 (~32% of all global deaths)**, of which **85% were due to
  heart attack and stroke** (WHO).
- **Stroke was the third leading cause of death and disability globally in 2021**, with
  **11.9 million new cases** and an estimated **93.8 million total cases**; **1 in 4 adults**
  is predicted to have a stroke in their lifetime (WHO).
- During a stroke *"every minute counts"* — **fast treatment lessens the brain damage a
  stroke can cause** (CDC). The same urgency applies to cardiac arrest: bystanders are often
  the first to act, and prompt CPR buys time until professionals arrive.

---

## 2. Check the scene & call for help (before any care)

The Red Cross "Giving CPR" sequence starts with safety and recognition:
1. **Check the scene for safety**, form an initial impression, and **use personal protective
   equipment (PPE)** if available.
2. If the person appears unresponsive, **check for responsiveness, breathing, life-threatening
   bleeding, or other life-threatening conditions** using **shout-tap-shout**.
3. If the person **does not respond and is not breathing or only gasping**, **CALL 9-1-1**
   and get equipment, or **tell someone to do so**.
4. Kneel beside the person; **place them on their back on a firm, flat surface**.

> **When in doubt, call 9-1-1 (or your local emergency number) and get help coming.** Minutes
> spent hesitating cost lives. Have someone call while you begin care.

## 3. Adult CPR — the Red Cross steps

CPR (cardiopulmonary resuscitation) helps keep blood moving to the brain and vital organs when
the heart stops or beats too ineffectively to circulate it. Perform **when someone is
unresponsive and not breathing or only gasping.**

The American Red Cross adult-CPR guidelines:
- **100 to 120 chest compressions per minute**, done **30 at a time**.
- **Hand position:** two hands centered on the chest.
- **Body position:** shoulders directly over hands; **elbows locked**.
- **Compression depth:** **at least 2 inches**.
- **Allow the chest to return to its normal position after each compression.**
- **Give 2 rescue breaths** after each set of 30: open the airway (head-tilt/chin-lift),
  pinch the nose shut, make a complete seal over the mouth, give a breath **lasting about
  1 second** that **makes the chest rise**; allow air to exit before the next breath.
  - *If a breath doesn't make the chest rise, re-tilt the head and reseal before the 2nd
    breath; if the 2nd still doesn't, an object may be blocking the airway.*
- **Continue sets of 30 compressions + 2 breaths.** Use an **AED as soon as one is available.**
- **Minimize interruptions to compressions to less than 10 seconds.**
- **Check for breathing for no more than 10 seconds** when you pause.

> **Pediatric note:** CPR for a child or baby differs (specialised care, smaller
> compressions/persistently different ratio). See a paediatric-CPR course — this doc is adult
> CPR from the Red Cross.

## 4. Hands-Only CPR (adult) — if you're untrained or unwilling to do breaths

Hands-only (compression-only) CPR is **simple, easy to learn, and for adults** — ideal for a
bystander:
1. **Have someone call 9-1-1 and get an AED.**
2. Ensure the person is **on their back on a firm, flat surface**.
3. **Kneel beside** them, knees about **shoulder-width apart**, near the body.
4. **Hand placement:** heel of **one hand in the center of the chest**, other hand on top;
   **interlace fingers up off the chest.**
5. **Body position:** shoulders **directly over hands**, **elbows locked**.
6. **Give continuous compressions** — push **hard and fast (at least 2 inches;
   100–120 compressions per minute)**; **allow the chest to recoil fully** each time.
7. Use an **AED as soon as it is available.**

> Hands-only CPR "can help gain precious time for a person experiencing a cardiac event." It
> is best on adults; for a child/baby, follow paediatric instruction.

## 5. Recognise a stroke — Act F.A.S.T. (CDC)

Call **9-1-1 immediately** if you see any of these stroke signs (especially **one-sided**):
- **Sudden numbness or weakness** in the face, arm, or leg;
- **Sudden confusion**, trouble speaking, or difficulty understanding speech;
- **Sudden trouble seeing** in one or both eyes;
- **Sudden trouble walking**, dizziness, loss of balance, or lack of coordination;
- **Sudden severe headache** with no known cause.

**Act F.A.S.T.** to test:
- **F — Face:** ask them to smile. Does **one side droop**?
- **A — Arms:** ask them to raise both arms. Does **one arm drift downward**?
- **S — Speech:** ask them to repeat a simple phrase. Is the speech **slurred or strange**?
- **T — Time:** if you see any of these signs, **call 9-1-1 right away**, and **note the time**
  symptoms first appeared.

> **Do not drive to the hospital** (and don't let the person drive). **Call 9-1-1 for an
> ambulance** so medical personnel can begin life-saving treatment on the way.
> The stroke treatments that work best are available only if the stroke is recognised and
> diagnosed **within 3 hours** of first symptoms.

**"Mini-stroke" (TIA):** if symptoms go away after a few minutes, it may be a transient
ischemic attack (TIA). Though brief, a **TIA is a sign of a serious condition** — tell a
health care team about the symptoms right away; don't dismiss it.

---

## 6. Limits & when to get professional care

- This is **recognition and first-response** knowledge. **Any life-threatening condition —
  suspected stroke, heart attack, severe bleeding, choking that doesn't clear — get
  professional emergency help immediately (9-1-1).**
- **Reading this is not training.** Take a certified first-aid/CPR/AED course and practise.
- **Never delay** calling for help while you read or search for information.
