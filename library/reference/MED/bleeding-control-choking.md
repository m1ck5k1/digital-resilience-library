# Medical & First Aid — Bleeding Control & Choking (Adult)

> Category: **MED** · Part: **Reference** (public)
> Primary source: **US Army FM 4-25.11 (FM 21-11) FIRST AID** — public-domain US Government work.
> Verified 2026-09-08 (PDF + `pdftotext`).
>
> ⚠ **Not medical advice.** This is universal first-response awareness derived from a US
> Department of Defense field manual — **not a substitute for professional medical care or for
> hands-on, in-person first aid training**. In any emergency, call emergency services and seek
> professional care. **Practice under qualified instruction.** This covers **adults only** —
> paediatric steps are not included (do not extrapolate adult steps to children).

The first two emergencies most likely to kill in the first minutes: **an airway you can't
clear**, and **bleeding you can't stop.** Both need fast, correct action.

---

## 1. Choking (adult) — FM 4-25.11 §2-10 to §2-12

- **Assess first:** ask the casualty if they can speak or are choking; check the **universal
  choking sign** (hands to throat).
- **Partial obstruction with good air exchange** (casualty can cough forcefully): **do NOT
  interfere** — encourage them to cough it up. Poor air exchange (weak cough, high-pitched noise,
  shock signs) → assist as a complete obstruction.
- **Complete obstruction** (can't speak, breathe, or cough): *"Once an obstructed airway occurs,
  the brain will develop an oxygen deficiency resulting in unconsciousness. Death will follow
  rapidly if breathing is not promptly restored."* → **call for help and immediately deliver
  manual thrusts.**

### How to do the thrusts
- **Abdominal thrust (Heimliech):** standing behind, wrap your arms around the waist; put your
  fist, thumb-side in, **midline, slightly above the navel and well below the tip of the
  breastbone**; grasp the fist with your other hand and press with a **quick backward-and-upward
  thrust**. **Each thrust is a separate, distinct movement**; continue until the object is
  expelled or the casualty becomes unresponsive.
- **Chest thrust** (hands centred on the middle of the breastbone) is used **only** for: advanced
  pregnancy, a markedly obese casualty, or a significant abdominal wound. Stand behind, arms under
  the armpits, fist thumb-side in the middle of the breastbone, **avoiding the breastbone tip and
  rib margins**; each thrust slow and distinct.
- **If the casualty becomes unresponsive:** call for help, open the airway, do a finger sweep, try
  rescue breathing; if still blocked, perform **6 to 10 manual thrusts**.

> ⚠ **No back blows:** the FM prescribes abdominal/chest thrusts only — it does **not** describe
> back slaps for adult choking, and no verified T1 back-blow source was found this pass. So this
> doc does not include them.

## 2. Severe bleeding control — FM 4-25.11 §2-13 to §2-20

*"The longer a service member bleeds from a major wound, the less likely he will be able to
survive."* Stop external bleeding promptly.

- **Entrance/exit wounds:** check for more than one wound — the **exit wound is usually larger**.
  If a missile/object is lodged, **do NOT remove it or probe the wound**; if an object is impaled,
  **build up a bulky dressing around it** to stabilise.

### The escalating steps (in order)
1. **Field dressing:** place the sterile side directly on the wound; wrap and tie a **nonslip knot
   over the outer edge of the dressing — not over the wound**; tie firmly enough not to slip **but
   not tourniquet-tight** (limb beyond shouldn't turn cool, blue, or numb).
2. **Manual pressure:** if bleeding continues, press firmly on the dressing for **5–10 minutes**.
   **Elevate the injured limb slightly above heart level** (unless you suspect a fracture that isn't
   splinted).
3. **Pressure dressing:** if it still bleeds, put a **wad of padding directly over the wound**, an
   improvised dressing/cravat **tightly wrapped around** it, tied **directly over the wound** —
   **again, not so tight it acts like a tourniquet**. Improvised bandages: strips of cloth
   (T-shirts, socks, garments).
4. **Digital pressure ("pressure points"):** press with fingers/thumbs where a main artery runs
   near the surface or over bone — used with direct pressure + elevation.
5. **Tourniquet — LAST RESORT:** *"use the tourniquet as a LAST RESORT,"* only on an **arm or leg**
   where there's a danger of bleeding to death (a soaked pressure dressing, major-artery bleeding,
   or traumatic amputation).

### Using a tourniquet (when the steps above fail)
- **Improve one:** strong, pliable material (bandages, clothing, cravats) **at least 2 inches
  wide** with a rigid stick-like object; **do NOT use wire or shoestring**.
- **Place** between the wound and the body/heart; **never directly over a wound, fracture, or
  joint**; best above the knee/upper arm; pad it well.
- **Apply:** tie a half-knot → place the stick on top → tie a full knot over it → **twist the stick
  until the bright-red bleeding stops** → fasten the free ends.
- **Once applied, do NOT loosen or release it** — releasing can precipitate shock. *"It must stay
  in place, and the casualty must be taken to [medical care] as soon as possible."* Leave it in
  full view; **mark a "T" and the time on the forehead**; treat for shock; seek medical aid. Only
  appropriately trained medical staff adjust/remove it.

## Limits & when to get care
- **Adults only** here — don't extrapolate to children. Call emergency services in any real
  emergency. **Reading is not training** — get hands-on first-aid/bleeding-control instruction.
- **Do not invent** tourniquet "loosening intervals" — the FM states none; the rule is *apply and
  leave it until medical care*.
