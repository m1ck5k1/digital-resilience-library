# Skills & Trades — Knots & Ropework

> Category: **LEARN** · Part: **Reference** (public)
> Knot steps from **Wikipedia** (CC BY-SA 4.0); cordage/lashing from **US Army FM 21-76**.
> Verified 2026-09-08.
> ⚠ **Not a substitute for hands-on practice.** A knot you haven't tied under load is a knot
> you don't know. **Critical:** never use a *binding* knot (like the square/reef knot) to join
> two ropes — use a proper bend.

---

## 1. The essentials

- A **knot** is used to secure a rope; a **bend** joins two ropes/lines together; a **hitch**
  secures a rope to an object. Get these right — the square knot (below) is a good *binding*
  knot but a **dangerous** bend.
- Natural-fibre **lashing/cordage** (FM 21-76): a quick **suitability test** — pull a length to
  test strength, twist fibres between your fingers; if it withstands handling, tie an overhand
  knot and gently tighten — if the knot doesn't break, the material is usable.

## 2. Knots (tied overhand / for a loop or binding)

### Bowline (fixed loop at the end of a rope)
- Forms a **fixed loop** at the end of a rope; easy to tie and untie — notably easy to untie
  **after** being under load. Called the *"king of the knots."*
- Used for a loop at one end of a line; the loop may pass around/through an object, tightening
  when loaded at the standing part. Can be tied **one-handed**.
- **Strength:** *"a rope with a bowline retains approximately 2/3 of its strength."*
- **Weaknesses:** tends to work loose when not under load (or under cyclic loading), can slip
  when pulled sideways, and the bight portion can capsize in certain circumstances.

### Sheet bend (joining two lines) — the proper bend
- A **bend knot** (square knot is **not** — see §3), also called the weaver's knot/hitch;
  practical for joining lines of **different diameter or rigidity**; quick and easy to tie; one
  of the six knots in the International Guild of Knot Tyers' Six Knot Challenge.
- For **unequal lines**, *double* the sheet bend (an extra round turn below the first, working end
  back under itself) for security; free ends should finish on the **same side** for most strength.
- **Security caveat:** a study of 8 bends with equal-diameter climbing rope found the sheet bend
  weak — *"in one test, it pulled apart with less than half the tension that other knots
  withstood."* Ashley: *"the sheet bend is the most practical of bends and quite secure enough for
  ordinary purposes."*

### Clove hitch (two single hitches around an object)
- *"Two successive single hitches tied around an object."* Most effective for securing the
  **middle section** of a rope to an object it crosses over; also an ordinary hitch/binding knot
  but *"not particularly secure in either application."*
- **Tying:** pass the running end around the spar and back over itself to form an X; pass it
  around the spar again, under the intersection of the last two turns, and pull both ends tight.
- Slips when loaded with certain cords; unreliable on a **square/rectangular** post vs a round one.
  Used in pioneering to start/finish lashings.

### Two half-hitches (mooring / securing)
- A binding/hitch; *"the commonest of all hitches for mooring."*
- **Tying:** (1) form a clockwise loop around the pole, working end on top, then bring the working
  end through the loop → an overhand knot around the pole; (2) bring the working end down and left,
  loop it under the standing end, pull it through the loop, tighten, and slide up to the post.
  A correctly tied two half-hitches resembles a clove hitch tied around the standing part.
- **Release:** pry the two hitches apart with a bending motion; it can be hard to untie — tie a
  *slipped* variation (pass through a bight in the second half-hitch) for easy release.

### Taut-line hitch (adjustable loop on a loaded line)
- An **adjustable loop** for lines under tension: tie a rolling hitch around the standing part
  after passing around an anchor; sliding the hitch adjusts the loop. Used for tent lines, tying
  down aircraft, adjustable moorings, securing loads.
- **Tying (#1856, the version most taught, incl. BSA):** (1) pass the working end around the
  anchor and bring it back alongside the standing part, make a half-hitch; (2) wrap again inside
  the loop (a round turn around the standing part); (3) finish with a half-hitch outside the loop,
  in the same direction, as for a clove hitch. Snug down, load slowly, adjust.
- **Security:** #1855 is *"considered the most secure"*; with stiff/slick modern fibres (e.g.
  polypropylene) these hitches *"can be difficult to make hold at all."*

## 3. ⚠ The square/reef knot is a BINDING knot — NEVER use it as a bend

- The **reef knot / square knot** (also Hercules knot) is an ancient, simple **binding** knot to
  secure a rope around an object: tie a left-handed overhand knot, then a right-handed overhand
  (*"right over left; left over right"*). Two consecutive overhands of the **same** handedness =
  a weak **granny** knot; working ends must emerge both top or both bottom, else a weak **thief**
  knot.
- **Critical caveat:** *"under no circumstances should it ever be tied as a bend"* — if the two
  ends are unequal, one stiffer/smoother, *"the knot is almost bound to spill."* The reef knot is
  *"not recommended for tying two ropes together, because of the potential instability… this has
  resulted in many accidental deaths."* Ashley claimed failures of misused reef knots *"have
  caused more deaths and injuries than failures of all other knots combined."*
- **Use a proper bend** (sheet bend, or a double fisherman's knot) to join two ropes instead.

## 4. Cordage & lashing (FM 21-76) — making your own binding material

- **Sinew** is the best natural lashing for **small objects** (from big-game tendons): dry fully,
  smash to separate fibres, moisten + twist into a continuous strand; **braid** strands for more
  strength. Moistened sinew is sticky and hardens when dry — so knots aren't even needed for small
  lashings.
- **Plant-fibre cord:** shred + braid the inner bark of linden, elm, hickory, white oak, mulberry,
  chestnut, red/white cedar; test strength; braid several strands.
- **Rawhide** for **larger** lashings (from medium/large game skins): remove excess fat/meat, dry
  completely (no folds), no need to remove hair; cut the dry skin into **~6 mm** strips (one
  continuous circular cut from the centre); soak **2–4 hours** until soft; use wet, stretching as
  much as possible — strong and durable when dry.
- A **cotton web belt** can be unravelled for strong string (fishing line, sewing thread, lashing).
