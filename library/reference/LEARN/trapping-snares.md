# Skills & Trades — Trapping & Snares

> Category: **LEARN** · Part: **Reference** (public)
> Primary source: **US Army FM 21-76** (Survival), Ch.8 "Food Procurement" — public-domain US
> Government work. Verified 2026-09-08.
> **Not a substitute for local hunting/trapping regulations or hands-on practice.** This is
> survival-trapping doctrine; **local wildlife law always applies** (the FM doesn't cover it).

Trapping is a good option *"for an unarmed survivor or evader, or when the sound of a rifle shot
is a problem"* — and *"several well-placed traps have the potential to catch **much more game than
a man with a rifle** is likely to shoot."*

---

## 1. Effectiveness depends on three things (FM 21-76)

To be effective with any trap/snare you must be:
- **Familiar with the species** you intend to catch,
- **Capable of constructing a proper trap**, and
- **Able to avoid alarming prey** by leaving signs of your presence.

*"There are no catchall traps"* — determine what species are in the area and set traps for them.

---

## 2. Where to set traps

- **Read the signs:** runs and trails, tracks, droppings, chewed/rubbed vegetation, nesting or
  roosting sites, and feeding/watering areas.
- A **trail** shows use by several species and is distinct; a **run** is smaller, less distinct,
  and carries signs of one species.
- **Place traps where there is proof animals pass through** — near bedding areas, waterholes, and
  feeding areas, on the trails linking them. *A perfect snare set haphazardly catches nothing.*
- **Concentrate on smaller animals** (abundant, easier to prepare) with dens/nests, fixed feeding
  areas, and connecting trails. Larger herding animals (elk, caribou) roam vast areas and are much
  harder to trap.

---

## 3. Concealment & scent control

- If you must dig, **remove all fresh dirt** (most animals avoid a pitfall-type trap).
- **Prepare trap parts away from the site**, then carry them in — avoids disturbing local vegetation.
- **Do not use freshly cut, live vegetation** — it "bleeds" sap whose odour alarms prey.
- **Remove or mask human scent** (mammals rely on smell more than sight): use fluid from the
  **gall/urine bladders of previous kills** (not human urine), **mud from an area with rotting
  vegetation**, or **smoke the trap parts**. If time permits, let the trap **weather a few days**
  (don't handle it while weathering). **Camouflage** the set trap.
- **Channelization:** funnel the trail toward the trap, **narrowest nearest the trap**, so the
  animal can't turn aside and walks in (few animals back up). Keep the constriction about the
  animal's body width, at least a body-length back from the trap.

### Bait
- Bait should be something the animal **knows** but that **isn't freely available close by**.
- Good small-mammal baits: **peanut butter** and **salt**; scatter bits so the prey "develops a
  craving" before reaching the trap.

---

## 4. Construction principles

Traps **crush, choke, hang, or entangle** — often combining two or more. Power comes from the
**struggling victim, gravity, or a bent sapling's tension.** *"The heart of any trap or snare is
the trigger."* **Snares are traps that incorporate a noose.**

### Simple snare
A noose over a trail or den hole, tied to a **firmly planted stake**; hold the noose open with
small twigs, grass, or **spider-web filaments** (excellent). Noose sized to pass freely over the
head; as the animal moves on it tightens on the neck — **the more it struggles, the tighter**.
Usually does **not** kill. **Wire is best** (cordage may slip off the neck).

### Drag noose
For an animal run: forked sticks either side + a sturdy **crossmember**; tie the noose to the
crossmember, hang it **above head height** (never low enough to step into). When the noose
tightens, the animal pulls the crossmember free and drags it; surrounding vegetation catches it.

### Twitch-Up snare
A **supple (hardwood) sapling bent over and held by a trigger.** Drive one forked-stick leg into
the ground; tie the twitch-up cordage to the second; engage the short legs so the bend holds;
extend a noose over the trail. When the animal catches its head it trips the forks, and the
twitch-up **springs up and hangs the prey**. **Do not use green sticks for the trigger** (sap can
glue them together).

### Squirrel pole
A long pole leaned against a tree in a high-squirrel area, with wire nooses along top and sides —
a squirrel going up/down must pass through one. Noose diameter **5–6 cm**, **~2.5 cm off the
pole**; **top/bottom nooses 45 cm from the pole ends** so the squirrel can't brace its feet (else
it chews the wire). Emplace **multiple** poles.

### Ojibwa bird pole
A centuries-old native snare, set in a **relatively open area** near feeding/dusting/watering
areas. Cut a pole **1.8–2.1 m**, trim limbs, **avoid resinous wood (pine)**. Sharpen the top;
drill a **small hole 5–7.5 cm down**; cut a **perch stick 10–15 cm long**. Tie a weight about the
**size of the target species** to cordage, pass the free end through the hole, tie a slip noose
over the perch. When a bird lands the perch falls, the weight drops, and the noose **tightens
around the bird's feet** (too heavy a weight cuts the feet off → escape).

### Noosing wand
A pole with a **slip noose of wire/stiff cordage** at the end; slip it over a roosting bird's neck
and pull, or place over a den hole from a blind and jerk when the animal emerges. **Requires a
patient operator.** Carry a stout club to kill the prey.

### Treadle spring snare
For small game on a trail: dig a shallow hole; set forked sticks either side, span with straight
sticks, and cover the hole so prey must step on at least one. Tie cordage to a twitch-up/raised
weight, route a noose, and set a **~5 cm trigger stick**. When the animal steps on a covering
stick, the noose catches it **by the foot**. **Channelization is required** (a disturbed trail =
wary animal).

### Figure-4 deadfall
A trigger that **drops a weight to crush** prey — weight must kill immediately. Made of **three
notched sticks** in a figure-4 under tension. **Practice beforehand** (close tolerances).

### Paiute deadfall
Like figure-4 but uses **cordage + a catch stick**; **easier to set.** When prey disturbs a
bait stick, the catch stick releases, the diagonal flies up, and the weight falls, crushing prey.

### ⚠ Bow trap
*"One of the **deadliest** traps… **dangerous to man as well as animals**."* An anchored bow with a
trip wire across the trail. **Note the danger** — this is not a beginner trap and poses a risk to
people.
