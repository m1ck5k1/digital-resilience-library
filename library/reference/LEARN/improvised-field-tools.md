# Skills & Trades — Improvised Field Tools

> Category: **LEARN** · Part: **Reference** (public)
> Primary source: **US Army FM 21-76** (Survival) — public-domain US Government work.
> Verified 2026-09-08.
> **Not a substitute for a proper, maintained tool.** Improvised tools are a survival fallback —
> they are harder to make and usually less durable.

The **knife is *"your most valuable tool in a survival situation"*** — keep it sharp and ready,
and if you must make a blade, know how.

---

## 1. Making a blade from natural materials

### Stone knife
- Need: a **sharp-edged stone** + a **chipping tool** (light, blunt-edged, to break off small
  pieces) + a **flaking tool** (pointed, to break off thin flat pieces — from bone, antler tines,
  or soft iron).
- Rough out the shape with the chipping tool; press the flaking tool against the edges to flake
  off the opposite side, leaving a razor edge. Lash the blade to a hilt.
- *"Stone will make an excellent puncturing tool and a good chopping tool but will not hold a fine
  edge. Some stones such as chert or flint can have very fine edges."*

### Bone knife
- Use larger bones (e.g. a deer leg bone); lay on a hard object and shatter with a heavy object;
  pick a pointed splinter; shape/sharpen by rubbing on rough-surfaced rock; lash to a hardwood
  handle.
- *"Use the bone knife only to puncture. It will not hold an edge and it may flake or break if used
  differently."*

### Wood knife
- Use only to puncture — *"bamboo is the only wood that will hold a suitable edge."*
- Pick straight-grained hardwood ~**30 cm long, 2.5 cm diameter**; fashion a ~**15 cm** blade;
  shave to a point; avoid the core/pith (weak point).
- **Fire hardening:** dry the blade over a fire slowly until lightly charred — *"the drier the
  wood, the harder the point"* — then sharpen on a coarse stone. For bamboo, char only the **inside**
  wood (the outer layer is hardest).

### Metal knife (best)
- Rub on a rough-surfaced stone for an edge. If the metal is soft enough, hammer out an edge cold
  on a flat hard anvil with a smaller hard hammer; make a handle from wood/bone.

### Other materials
- **Glass** has a natural edge but is less durable for heavy work; **plastic** can be sharpened into
  a durable point if thick/hard enough.

## 2. Spears & arrow points

- **Spear:** make a blade like a knife; select a straight sapling shaft **1.2–1.5 m**; attach the
  blade by lashing (preferred: split the handle, insert the blade, wrap tightly). Or shave a straight
  hardwood shaft **1.2–1.5 m** to a point and fire-harden. **Bamboo spear:** choose **1.2–1.5 m**;
  from **8–10 cm** back of the point, shave down at a **45° angle**; sharpen only the inner portion.
- **Arrow points:** same as a stone-knife blade; chert, flint, and shell-type stones are best; bone
  can be flaked like stone; broken glass can make efficient points.

---

## 3. Clubs, the throwing stick & the bola

- **Clubs (three basic types):**
  - **Simple** — a straight-grained hardwood staff/branch, diameter that fits comfortably in the palm.
  - **Weighted** — a weight lashed on one end (a natural knot or stone). A stone with a slight
    hourglass shape works well; shape by "pecking" (rap with a smaller hard stone); lash with a
    split-handle, forked-branch, or wrapped-handle technique.
  - **Sling club** — a weight hangs **8–10 cm** from the handle on strong flexible lashing.
- **Throwing stick (rabbit stick):** a blunt stick naturally curved at about **45°**; choose heavy
  hardwood such as oak; shave two opposite sides flat like a boomerang; requires practice.
- **Bola:** easy to make; effective for capturing running game or low-flying fowl in a flock. Hold
  the centre knot, twirl above the head, release — the weighted cords separate and wrap/immobilise
  the target.

## 4. Cordage & lashing (natural binding)

- **Suitability test:** pull a length; twist fibres between fingers; if it withstands handling, tie
  an overhand knot and tighten — if it doesn't break, it's usable.
- **Sinew** (best for small objects) — from big-game tendons: dry fully, smash to separate fibres,
  moisten + twist into a continuous strand; braid for strength. Moistened sinew is sticky and hardens
  dry, so **knots aren't needed** for small lashings.
- **Plant-fibre cord** — shred + braid inner bark of linden, elm, hickory, white oak, mulberry,
  chestnut, red/white cedar; test strength; braid several strands.
- **Rawhide** (for larger lashings) — from medium/large game skins: remove excess fat/meat, dry
  completely (no folds), no need to remove hair; cut the dry skin into **~6 mm** strips (a continuous
  circular cut from the centre); soak **2–4 hours** until soft; use wet, stretching as much as
  possible — strong and durable when dry.
- A **cotton web belt** can be unravelled into strong string (fishing line, sewing thread, lashing).
