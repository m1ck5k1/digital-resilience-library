## 1. NOAA Weather Radio (NWR) — the free, always-on backbone

- NWR is a *"nationwide network of radio stations broadcasting continuous weather information
  directly from the nearest National Weather Service office"* — **24 hours a day, 7 days a
  week**, and it works with the FCC's **Emergency Alert System (EAS)** as an **"All Hazards"**
  network. Provided as a **public service by NOAA**.
- **Coverage:** *"more than 1,000 transmitters, covering all 50 states, adjacent coastal waters,
  Puerto Rico, the U.S. Virgin Islands, and the U.S. Pacific Territories."*
- It **requires a special receiver/scanner** capable of the signal.
- **The seven VHF "WX" frequencies (MHz):** **162.400 · 162.425 · 162.450 · 162.475 · 162.500 ·
  162.525 · 162.550.**
- **Alerts:** during an emergency, "NWS forecasters interrupt routine broadcasts and send a
  **special tone**" that activates weather radios set to alert. Hazards covered: weather, natural,
  technological (chemical/oil/nuclear), and national emergencies. For non-weather events, NWS
  activates **at the request of local/state officials**.

## 2. Amateur ("ham") radio

- Ham radio *"brings people, electronics and communication together… talk across town, around the
  world, or even into space, all without the Internet or cell phones"* — and *"can be a lifeline
  during times of need."*
- **Emergency role:** when regular channels fail, hams assist emergency communications — e.g. the
  Amateur Radio Service kept NYC agencies in touch after their command center was destroyed in 9/11.
- **Licensing:** you must **pass an FCC exam** and hold an **FCC (or Canadian) amateur licence** to
  operate on the amateur bands. **27 small frequency bands** are allocated to the service
  internationally; **all frequencies are shared** (none is exclusive).
- Governed by **47 C.F.R. Part 97**.

## 3. Family radio (FRS), GMRS, and CB — no-license vs licensed

### FRS (Family Radio Service) — no individual licence
- *"A private, two-way, **very short-distance** voice and data communications service"* — *"similar
  to walkie-talkies"* — for family/group activities.
- **Licensed by rule:** an individual licence is **not required** if you comply with the rules (Part
  95 Subpart B).
- Current law (**eCFR**): FRS is allotted **22 channels**, each **12.5 kHz** bandwidth, all shared
  with GMRS. Frequencies: ch 1–7 **462.5625–462.7125 MHz**; ch 8–14 **467.5625–467.7125 MHz**;
  ch 15–22 **462.5500–462.7250 MHz**.
- **Power:** ERP **≤ 0.5 W** on ch 8–14; **≤ 2.0 W** on ch 1–7 and 15–22.

### GMRS (General Mobile Radio Service) — licensed
- *"A **licensed** radio service that uses channels around **462 MHz and 467 MHz**"* for
  *"short-distance, two-way communication… of licensees and their immediate family members."*
  Eligibility for new licences is **individuals** only (Part 95 Subpart A).

### CB (Citizens Band) — no individual licence
- **40 channels**, ch 1 ≈ **26.965 MHz** … ch 40 ≈ **27.405 MHz** (26.965–27.405 MHz).
- **Priority:** *"at all times and on all channels, give priority to emergency communications…
  Channel 9 may be used only for emergency communications or traveler assistance."*
- **Power:** AM/FM mean carrier **≤ 4 W**; SSB peak envelope **≤ 12 W**.
- **Antenna height:** antenna not above **18.3 m (60 ft)** off the ground, or **6.1 m (20 ft)**
  above the building/tree it's mounted on, whichever is higher. External RF power amplifiers are
  prohibited.

## 4. Text-over-call & out-of-area contact (the universal principle)

From the American Red Cross "Make a Plan": *"It may be easier to **text or call long distance** if
local phone lines are overloaded or out of service."* So:
- Choose an **out-of-area contact** — long-distance/text frequently works when local lines are
  congested.
- **Everyone should carry emergency contact info in writing AND saved on their phone.**
- Reconnecting: register on the Red Cross **Safe and Well** site, or call **1-800-RED CROSS
  (1-800-733-2767)** and select "Disaster."
