# Navigation — Dead Reckoning & Pacing

> Category: **NAV** · Part: **Reference** (public)
> Primary source: **US Army FM 3-25.26** (Map Reading and Land Navigation, 20 Jul 2001) —
> public-domain US Government work. Verified 2026-09-08.
> **Not a substitute for hands-on practice with your own compass and on your own terrain.**

This covers the **distance + route-following technique**: knowing how far you've walked and
following a line. It builds on the azimuth/compass (see `map-compass-lost.md`) and natural-navigation
(see `natural-navigation.md`) docs — this is "how far + how to walk it."

---

## 1. Pace count — your personal ground-truth ruler

- A **pace** = one natural step, about **30 inches (≈0.76 m)**.
- To use the method you must know **your own pace count: how many paces you take to walk
  100 meters.**
- Find it by walking an accurately measured course; the course can be **100–600 m**, and it
  **must be on similar terrain to what you will walk** (a flat-terrain count must not be used on
  hilly ground).
- On a **600 m** course: count total paces, **divide by 6** → your average paces per 100 m.
- **Track** distance with a **pebble moved per 100 m**, **knots in a string**, or **marks in a
  notebook** — *"do not try to remember the count."*

### Factors that change your pace length
| Factor | Effect |
|--------|--------|
| **Slopes** | Pace lengthens **downslope**, shortens **uphill** (e.g. 120 flat → 130+ uphill). |
| **Winds** | **Head wind** shortens; **tail wind** lengthens. |
| **Surfaces** | Sand, gravel, mud, snow shorten the pace. |
| **Elements** | Falling snow, rain, ice reduce pace length. |
| **Clothing** | Excess clothing / poor-traction boots affect pace. |
| **Visibility** | Poor visibility (fog, rain, dark) shortens the pace. |

## 2. Estimating distance & time without pacing

- **Normal rate** for an 8-hour foot march is **4 kmph**, varying with distance/time/terrain/
  weather/load.
- **100-metre unit-of-measure:** up to **500 m**, count the 100 m increments between objects;
  **beyond 500 m**, count to a halfway point and **double** it.
- **Flash-to-bang** (range to an explosion/shot): count seconds from the flash until you hear the
  report, **multiply by 330 m** (soldiers often use 350 m).

## 3. Dead reckoning — the method

**Dead reckoning = moving a set distance along a set line.** Two steps:
1. On the map, use a protractor + graphic scales to determine the **direction and distance**.
2. On the ground, use a **compass + a means of measuring distance** (pacing, odometer, or elapsed
   time) to apply it.

**Key principles (FM 3-25.26):**
- Between position-fixes, establish location by **measuring/estimating distance along the azimuth
  from the last known point** — via pacing, odometer, or elapsed time.
- Most routes are **not single straight lines** — compasses and pace-counts are imprecise and
  error compounds over distance. Plan DR routes as a **series of straight-line distances between
  checkpoints**, reconfirming position by terrain association or resection.
- **Advantages:** easy to learn; accurate over short distances. **Disadvantages:** errors
  accumulate the farther you go without confirming position; needs constant compass attention;
  every detour complicates it.

### Steering marks
- **Never walk with the compass open in front of you** (it won't stay level). Instead: face the
  azimuth, **sight a distant landmark on it (a "steering mark")**, close the compass, walk there,
  repeat.
- Select steering marks **during the march** (never from map study), on/near the **highest visible
  points** along the azimuth. Best = the **most distant** clearly-distinguishable object; when in
  doubt pick the highest/most distant so you walk farther between checks.
- **At night**, marks need more unique shapes (colours disappear → silhouettes). With no marks /
  poor visibility, send a member forward as your own steering mark.

### Detours
- Return to the **original line** as soon as terrain permits.
- To turn **right 90° add 90°** to your azimuth; **left 90° subtract 90°**.
- **Count only paces toward the destination** — not the perpendicular paces used to detour and get
  back on line.

### The deliberate (intentional) offset
- When the destination lies on a **large linear feature running perpendicular to your travel**
  (road, railroad, power line, ridge, stream), apply a **deliberate ~10° error** to your azimuth,
  walk until you hit the feature — then you know exactly which way to turn to find the destination.
- This is advised over blind precision: it copes with compass error and magnetic variation.

### Position confirmation
- **Restart your pace count each time your position is reconfirmed on the map.** Periodically do a
  **resection (triangulation from two or more known points)** to correct drift.
